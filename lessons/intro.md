## The Need for Pinyin

The Chinese language is written with characters called <py>hànzì</py>.
In order to read and write Chinese, you will have to memorize many
<py>hànzì</py>.

However, in order to begin speaking Chinese, you will need to learn
how the characters are pronounced.  In modern China, the pronunciation
of characters is indicated using a romanization system called
_pinyin_.  The word _romanization_ means that the letters of
the Roman alphabet are used; the word <py>pīnyīn</py> is Chinese for
"spell the sounds".

## The History of Pinyin

The _pinyin_ romanization scheme was invented in mainland China
in the 1950s.  The original purpose of _pinyin_ was to promote a
common dialect that everyone could use; this dialect is often called
Mandarin.  Since different dialects pronounce the characters in
different ways, there needed to be a way to indicate pronunciation
independent of the characters.  A committee organized by the
government considered many different schemes before selecting
_pinyin_.

Because _pinyin_ was initially intended for native Chinese to
use, the committee didn't always use the letters to represent the same
sounds as in other languages.  So, you can't complain that the letter
<py>c</py>, for instance, sounds like the combination _ts_ in
English.  That would be like learning German and complaining that
their _v_ sounds like an English _f_ and their _w_
sounds like an English _v_.  To learn a foreign language, you
have to learn how it uses the letters for its own purposes.

The committee also made some other decisions that would make
_pinyin_ more beneficial to native Chinese, especially speakers
of dialects other than Mandarin.  We will see some of these decisions
throughout the lessons that follow.

## The Sound System

A language like English can have thousands of different syllables.
Mandarin Chinese, however, has only about 400.  This is because the
sound system of the language restricts what sounds can go together.
Many people find it easiest to learn the sound system of Chinese by
looking at the syllabic structure.

Traditionally, a Chinese syllable is divided into three parts.  The
first part is called the _initial_; it is the beginning consonant
of the syllable.  If the syllable doesn't have a beginning consonant,
we say the initial is _empty_.

The second part of the syllable is what comes after the initial; it is
called the _final_.  All Chinese syllables have a final.  There
is always a main vowel; sometimes a sound such as <py>i</py> or
<py>u</py> comes before it, and sometimes another vowel or <py>n</py>
or <py>ng</py> comes after it.

The third part of the syllable is called the _tone_.  When you
speak Chinese, the pitch of your voice will change with every
syllable.  Learning the tone of a syllable is just as important as
learning which syllable of an English word to put the stress on.
People will not be able to figure out what you're saying from context
if you don't get the tones right, just as you would have trouble
understanding someone speaking English with the accent on the wrong
syllables.

## The Structure of Pinyin

In the 1950s, the government of the People's Republic of China formed
a committee to come up with a way to indicate the pronunciation of
Mandarin Chinese using some sort of alphabet.  This committee
eventually decided to use the Roman alphabet.  In adapting it to the
sounds of Chinese, they adopted some basic principles to use as
guidelines.

One basic principle was that each sound should be represented by one
symbol, and that each symbol should represent just one sound.  The
consonants obey this rule perfectly; you never have a situation as in
English, where the letter _c_ can have three different sounds,
usually depending on what letter follows.  However, there weren't
enough consonant symbols to go around, so three sounds are represented
by the consonant pairs <py>zh</py>, <py>ch</py>, and <py>sh</py>.

Unfortunately, the vowels aren't as obedient: each vowel has at least
two different pronunciations.  However, you can always determine the
correct pronunciation by what letters surround the vowel; for
instance, the <py>e</py> in <egpy>en</egpy> is pronounced differently
than the <py>e</py> in <egpy>ye</egpy>, but the combinations
<egpy>en</egpy> and <egpy>ye</egpy> never vary in pronunciation
themselves.

Because of this, it is suggested that you don't try to learn all the
rules of sound change for the individual vowels.  Instead, learn the
clusters as a unit, using the vowels you see to remind you of the
pronunciation.  In essence, you will be learning how to pronounce the
initials and the finals.

Tones in Chinese are indicated by marks over the main vowel of a
syllable.  There are four tones in Mandarin Chinese; the four tones of
the syllable <py>o</py> would be marked as follows:  <py>ō</py>
<py>ó</py> <py>ǒ</py> <py>ò</py>.  Using marks makes it look like the
tones are secondary or not as important as the letters.  However,
remember that getting the tone right is just as important as getting
the accent right in English.  Of course, we don't mark the accent in
English, so foreigners have to struggle with this quite a bit.  Be
glad that the Chinese mark the tones for you!
