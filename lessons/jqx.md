## Explanation (1)

Say the syllable <egpy>yi</egpy>.  Notice where the tip of your tongue
is: touching right behind your lower teeth.  Notice also that your
tongue bends up and seems to touch the roof of your mouth.

Keeping your tongue in this position, try making an <py>sh</py> sound.
This is the sound of the _pinyin_ <py>x</py>.  At first, it will
be very difficult to distinguish it from an <py>sh</py>.  But there is
a rule that you might find helpful:

**The initials** <py>j q x</py> **combine only with finals
beginning with** <py>y</py>**.**

## Listening Practice (1a)

Syllables beginning with <py>x</py> are affected by the following
spelling rules:

**Silent** <py>y</py> **disappears.**  _Example:_
<py>x</py> followed by <egpy>yi</egpy> becomes <egpy>xi</egpy>.

**Pronounced** <py>y</py> **becomes** <py>i</py>**.**
_Example:_ <py>x</py> followed by <egpy>ya</egpy> becomes
<egpy>xia</egpy>.

<py>you</py> **becomes** <py>iu</py>**.**  _Example:_
<py>x</py> followed by <egpy>you</egpy> becomes <egpy>xiu</egpy>.

## Explanation (2)

The initials <py>j q x</py> are complements to the initials <py>zh ch
sh</py>.  Just as <py>x</py> is pronounced like <py>sh</py> but with
the tip of the tongue right behind the lower teeth, <py>j</py> and
<py>q</py> are pronounced like <py>zh</py> and <py>ch</py>,
respectively, but with the tongue right behind the lower teeth.

You may also find it helpful to think of <py>j</py> as <py>d</py> +
<py>x</py> and <py>q</py> as <py>t</py> + <py>x</py>.

Here are some principles that may be useful:

**1. The difference between** <py>j q</py> **and** <py>zh
ch</py> **is tongue position.**  For <py>j q</py> the tip of the
tongue is behind the lower teeth; for <py>zh ch</py> the tip of the
tongue curls up.

**2. The difference between** <py>j zh</py> **and** <py>q
ch</py> **is aspiration.**  A puff of air or an _h_ sound
comes with <py>q ch</py> but not with <py>j zh</py>.

**3.** <py>j q x</py> **combine only with finals beginning
with** <py>y</py>; <py>zh ch sh</py> **never combine with finals
beginning with** <py>y</py>**.**

**Note:** The initial <py>q</py> does not sound at all like the
English letter _q_.  Listen to the difference between these two
syllables: <egpy>quan</egpy> <egpy>kuan</egpy>.  Never use <py>qu</py>
to represent the English _qu_ sound; use <py>ku</py> instead.

## Explanation (3)

We've mentioned that <py>j q x</py> combine with finals beginning with
<py>y</py>.  That includes the four finals beginning with <py>yu</py>,
namely <egpy>yu</egpy>, <egpy>yue</egpy>, <egpy>yuan</egpy>, and
<egpy>yun</egpy>.

**When one of** <py>yu yue yuan yun</py> **follows one of**
<py>j q x</py>**, the** <py>y</py> **is dropped.**

&nbsp;<py>j</py> + <egpy>yu</egpy> &#x2192; <egpy>ju</egpy>

&nbsp;<py>q</py> + <egpy>yuan</egpy> &#x2192; <egpy>quan</egpy>

&nbsp;<py>x</py> + <egpy>yun</egpy> &#x2192; <egpy>xun</egpy>

Perhaps you can see the confusion this causes:

&nbsp;<py>u</py> can come from <egpy>wu</egpy> or <egpy>yu</egpy>

&nbsp;<py>uan</py> can come from <egpy>wan</egpy> or <egpy>yuan</egpy>

&nbsp;<py>un</py> can come from <egpy>wen</egpy> or <egpy>yun</egpy>

Some students first learning _pinyin_ will write
<egpy>juan</egpy> when they hear <egpy>zhuan</egpy> and will write
<egpy>chun</egpy> when they hear <egpy>qun</egpy>.  This is not
unexpected, since English only has _j_ and _ch_, while
Chinese has both <py>j q</py> and <py>zh ch</py>.

**When** <py>u</py> **follows** <py>j q x</py>, **it must be
thought of as** <py>yu</py>**.  If an initial precedes the**
<egpy>yu</egpy> **sound, it must be one of** <py>j q x</py>**.**

Also note that <egpy>jue</egpy> rhymes with <eg src="eh">eh</eg> and
<egpy>zhui</egpy> rhymes with <eg src="yay">yay</eg>.  <egpy>jue</egpy> and
<egpy>zhui</egpy> do not rhyme with each other.
